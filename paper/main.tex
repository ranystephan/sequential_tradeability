\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Many candidate alpha signals look profitable in a backtest but fail once estimation
uncertainty, alpha decay, and implementation costs are taken into account.  We model
alpha validation as a sequential decision problem: a researcher observes a noisy
evidence process whose unknown drift is the signal's strength and chooses, at each
instant, whether to continue observing, activate the signal, or reject it.  The
filtering state follows the Bayesian drift-estimation framework of Ekstr\"om, Karatzas,
and Vaicenavicius (2022).  We replace their statistical terminal loss with an
economic tradeability payoff (risk-adjusted profit net of an implementation hurdle and
a false-discovery penalty), and an optimal policy is given by the free boundary of a
variational inequality that we solve numerically.  On 192 anomaly portfolios from the
Open Source Asset Pricing dataset, the rule activates 72.9\% of real signals but only
27.1\% of drift-destroyed placebos, and it decides in 48 months on average.  Its
activation-weighted post-decision returns are higher in point estimate than those of
placebo-calibrated fixed-horizon tests at 60, 120, and 240 months.  A stock-level
CRSP/Compustat reconstruction of three canonical signals demonstrates the pipeline on
raw data and shows how sharply admission decisions depend on transaction-cost
assumptions.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} alpha validation, sequential analysis, optimal stopping,
Bayesian filtering, multiple testing, transaction costs.

\section{Introduction}

Quantitative researchers face a steady stream of candidate alpha signals, most of
which are not worth trading.  The academic anomaly literature alone contains hundreds
of published predictors.  The standard response to this multiplicity has been
statistical: raise the $t$-statistic hurdle to account for data mining
\citep{harveyLiuZhu2016}, discount backtests for overfitting
\citep{baileyBorweinLopezdePradoZhu2017}, and expect post-publication returns to decay
\citep{mcleanPontiff2016}.  These corrections have a common structure.  They treat
validation as a fixed-horizon hypothesis test: collect a sample of given length,
compute a statistic, compare it with a threshold.

This paper starts from a different premise: alpha validation is a sequential
real-options problem, not a fixed-horizon test.  Continuing to observe a candidate
signal has option value, because future evidence may turn an ambiguous signal into a
tradeable one.  But delay is costly, because research capacity is scarce and deployable
alpha decays.  A natural formalization is optimal stopping.  At each point in time,
the researcher compares the value of stopping now with the value of paying the
observation cost and learning more.  Stopping means activating the signal if its
risk-adjusted posterior value clears an implementation hurdle, and rejecting it
otherwise.

We build this formalization in three steps.  First, we model accumulated evidence as a
Brownian motion whose unknown drift is the signal's true strength, and we adopt the
Bayesian filtering framework of \citet{ekstromKaratzasVaicenavicius2022} (henceforth
EKV).  In this framework the posterior mean and variance form the decision state, and
the posterior variance plays a dual role: it measures what remains unknown and sets
the speed at which learning proceeds.  Second, we replace EKV's statistical terminal
loss with an economic tradeability payoff: the optimized mean-variance value of
trading at the posterior belief, net of a fixed implementation hurdle, with the option
to reject.  Because the reward changes, the EKV optimality results no longer apply.
We therefore solve the new finite-horizon obstacle problem numerically and validate
the solver against the closed-form benchmarks that the EKV framework provides.  Third,
we introduce a three-state dead/alive prior and a false-discovery penalty, charged in
posterior expectation when a dead signal is admitted.  The motivation comes from
simulation: a Gaussian prior assigns zero probability to a truly dead signal and
therefore over-activates pure noise.

The empirical question is whether the sequential rule earns its complexity on real
data.  We test it on monthly long-short returns for 192 anomaly portfolios from the
Open Source Asset Pricing dataset \citep{chenZimmermann2022}, against placebos that
destroy directional drift while preserving each signal's volatility profile.  The rule
separates signal from noise, activating 72.9\% of real signals against 27.1\% of
placebos.  Its sequential structure also pays.  Against fixed-horizon posterior tests
calibrated to the same placebo activation count, it comes within ten points of a
240-month test's activation power while deciding in 48 months on average, and its
gains in activation-weighted post-decision returns over the 60-, 120-, and 240-month
benchmarks are positive throughout, though imprecisely estimated.  A stock-level
CRSP/Compustat reconstruction of momentum, book-to-market, and operating profitability
then demonstrates the pipeline on raw data: all three are admitted on gross returns,
and none survives a deliberately punitive cost stress test.  The contrast previews how
much the admission decision depends on the cost model.

\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:deadalive} develop the model;
Section~\ref{sec:empirics} contains the real-data experiments;
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The mathematical machinery comes from sequential analysis.  The idea that evidence
should be collected until it is decisive, rather than for a fixed span, goes back to
the sequential probability ratio test of \citet{wald1945}; continuous-time,
free-boundary formulations are developed in \citet{shiryaev2008} and
\citet{peskirShiryaev2006}.  Our state process is taken directly from
\citet{ekstromKaratzasVaicenavicius2022}, who characterize optimal stopping rules for
Bayesian least-squares estimation of a Wiener drift.  We preserve their filtering
structure but change the terminal reward from estimation accuracy to economic
tradeability, which yields a new obstacle problem.

The application speaks to the factor-validation literature.
\citet{harveyLiuZhu2016} argue that multiple testing requires substantially higher
$t$-statistic hurdles for new factors; \citet{mcleanPontiff2016} document
post-publication decay of anomaly returns; \citet{baileyBorweinLopezdePradoZhu2017}
quantify backtest overfitting.  These papers adjust the \emph{threshold} of a
fixed-horizon test.  Our contribution is to make the \emph{horizon} itself a choice
variable and to price the decision in economic units (risk-adjusted profit net of
an implementation hurdle and a false-discovery penalty) rather than in test statistics.
On the cost side, \citet{novyMarxVelikov2016} find that cost mitigation leaves many
lower-turnover anomalies with significant net spreads but few higher-turnover ones,
and \citet{chenVelikov2023} find that the average anomaly's net expected return is
close to zero; our stock-level feasibility check illustrates the same sensitivity
through the admission decision.

\section{Sequential Evidence Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Filtering state}

Let $X$ denote the unknown strength of a candidate alpha signal.  Evidence accumulates
as the observation process
\begin{equation}
    Y_t = X\,t + W_t, \qquad t \ge 0,
    \label{eq:observation_model}
\end{equation}
where $W$ is a standard Brownian motion independent of $X$, and $X$ has prior
distribution $\mu$ with finite second moment.  In the alpha-validation reading, $Y_t$
is cumulative normalized evidence, for instance cumulative risk-scaled long-short
return, and $X$ is an annualized Sharpe-like strength; Section~\ref{sec:osap_protocol}
makes this normalization precise for monthly returns.  Writing
$\mathcal F_t^Y=\sigma(Y_s:0\le s\le t)$ for the observation filtration, Bayes' rule
gives the posterior
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{t,y}(du)
    =
    \frac{\exp\{uy-u^2t/2\}\,\mu(du)}
    {\int_{\mathbb R}\exp\{vy-v^2t/2\}\,\mu(dv)},
    \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
with posterior mean $G(t,y)=\int u\,\mu_{t,y}(du)$ and posterior variance
$H(t,y)=\int(u-G(t,y))^2\mu_{t,y}(du)$, so that
$\widehat X_t=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal F_t^Y]=G(t,Y_t)$.

The innovation process $\widetilde W_t=Y_t-\int_0^t\widehat X_s\,ds$ is a Brownian
motion in $\mathcal F_t^Y$.  Differentiating the exponential tilt in
\eqref{eq:posterior} gives $\partial_yG(t,y)=H(t,y)>0$ for non-degenerate priors, so
$G_t=G(t,\cdot)$ is invertible on the range of posterior means and
\begin{equation}
    d\widehat X_t = \Psi(t,\widehat X_t)\,d\widetilde W_t,
    \qquad
    \Psi(t,x) = H\bigl(t,G_t^{-1}(x)\bigr).
    \label{eq:posterior_mean_sde}
\end{equation}
The posterior mean is therefore an $\mathcal F_t^Y$-martingale, and the same function
$\Psi$ is also the conditional variance,
$\Psi(t,\widehat X_t)=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal F_t^Y)$.  This dual role gives
the stopping problem its transparent structure: posterior uncertainty is simultaneously
the remaining estimation error and the local volatility of learning.

\subsection{The EKV benchmark and two closed-form cases}
\label{sec:ekv}

EKV study the statistical stopping problem
$\inf_{\tau}\mathbb E[(X-\widehat X_\tau)^2+c\tau]$, where $c>0$ is the observation
cost per unit time.  Their analysis rests on the following identity
\citep[Proposition~3.1]{ekstromKaratzasVaicenavicius2022}.

\begin{proposition}[Variance decay]
For any stopping time $\tau$ of $\mathcal F^Y$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E\bigl[\Psi(\tau,\widehat X_\tau)\bigr]
    =
    \operatorname{Var}(X)
    -
    \mathbb E\left[\int_0^\tau \Psi^2(s,\widehat X_s)\,ds\right].
    \label{eq:variance_decay_identity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\noindent
Since $\mathbb E[(X-\widehat X_\tau)^2]=\mathbb E[\Psi(\tau,\widehat X_\tau)]$, the EKV
objective is equivalent, up to the constant $\operatorname{Var}(X)$, to
$\inf_\tau\mathbb E\int_0^\tau(c-\Psi^2(s,\widehat X_s))\,ds$.  The interpretation is
direct: one more instant of observation costs $c$ and reduces posterior variance at
rate $\Psi^2$.  An optimal rule is not myopic, however.  In the two-point case below,
continuation extends into states where $c$ already exceeds $\Psi^2$, because the
posterior mean may return to the fast-learning region near zero.

Two priors admit closed forms, and we use both as exact benchmarks for our numerical
solver.  Under a Gaussian prior $X\sim N(m_0,q_0)$,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat X_t=\frac{m_0+q_0Y_t}{1+q_0t},
    \qquad
    \Psi(t,x)=\frac{q_0}{1+q_0t},
    \qquad
    \tau^\star=\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt c}-\tfrac{1}{q_0}\Bigr)^{\!+},
    \label{eq:gaussian_benchmark}
\end{equation}
so the posterior variance is deterministic and an optimal stopping time is the
constant $\tau^\star$.  Under a two-point prior on $\{-\beta,\beta\}$, the posterior
variance function is $\Psi(x)=\beta^2-x^2$, and immediate stopping is optimal when
$\beta^4\le c$.  Otherwise an optimal rule is the first exit
$\tau_b^\star=\inf\{t\ge0:|\widehat X_t|\ge b\}$, where $\gamma=\sqrt{\beta^2-\sqrt c}$
is the state at which $\Psi^2=c$ and $b$ is the unique root in $(\gamma,\beta)$ of
$\int_0^b\bigl[c-(\beta^2-\xi^2)^2\bigr](\beta^2-\xi^2)^{-2}d\xi=0$.  That $b$ exceeds
$\gamma$ is the non-myopic continuation noted above.  The two-point
case already has the shape of an alpha admission rule: continue while the posterior
mean sits in a band around zero and stop once it exits, the continuous-time analogue
of Wald's two-threshold test.

\section{A Tradeability Stopping Problem}
\label{sec:tradeability}

\subsection{The activation payoff}

EKV's terminal reward is statistical accuracy; an alpha researcher cares about money.
We therefore replace the reward with an economic payoff while keeping the filtering
state; none of the EKV optimality theory then carries over, and the finance problem
must be solved on its own.

Suppose that at the moment of stopping the posterior belief about the signal strength
has mean $m$ and variance $q$.  If the signal is activated with signed exposure $w$, we
evaluate it by the posterior predictive mean-variance objective
\begin{equation}
    m w-\frac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(\sigma_r^2+q\bigr)w^2-\kappa,
    \label{eq:activation_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ is risk aversion, $\sigma_r^2$ is the residual return variance of the
traded signal, and $\kappa\ge0$ is an implementation hurdle covering spread, impact,
and the opportunity cost of research capacity.  The term $\sigma_r^2+q$ is predictive
risk: the position is penalized both for return noise and for uncertainty about the
latent alpha itself, expressed in the same annualized units as $m$.  Optimizing over
$w$ and retaining the option to reject gives the terminal payoff
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(m,q)
    =
    \left(\frac{m^2}{2\lambda(\sigma_r^2+q)}-\kappa\right)^{\!+},
    \label{eq:tradeability_payoff}
\end{equation}
attained by $w^\star=m/\bigl(\lambda(\sigma_r^2+q)\bigr)$ when the net value is
positive.  Immediate activation therefore requires
\begin{equation}
    |m|>\sqrt{2\lambda(\sigma_r^2+q)\kappa}.
    \label{eq:activation_threshold}
\end{equation}
This is only a terminal threshold: before the validation deadline, an ambiguous signal
also carries option value, because more evidence may push the posterior mean into the
tradeable region.

\subsection{The finite-horizon obstacle problem}
\label{sec:vi}

Under the Gaussian prior the posterior variance is the deterministic function
$q(t)=q_0/(1+q_0t)$ and the posterior mean follows $dm_t=q(t)\,d\widetilde W_t$.  Given
a validation horizon $T$, an observation cost $c$, and a decay rate $\rho\ge0$, the
tradeability value is
\begin{equation}
    V(t,m)
    =
    \sup_{\tau\in[t,T]}
    \mathbb E_{t,m}
    \left[
        e^{-\rho\tau}\,\Phi\bigl(m_\tau,q(\tau)\bigr)-\int_t^\tau c\,ds
    \right].
    \label{eq:tradeability_value}
\end{equation}
Value is measured in calendar time from the start of validation, so the discount
$e^{-\rho\tau}$ sits inside the obstacle; the equivalent current-time formulation uses
$e^{-\rho(\tau-t)}$ and a $-\rho V$ term in the continuation equation.  The decay term
is economically essential.  Because the payoff \eqref{eq:tradeability_payoff} is convex
in $m$, the undiscounted problem can call for continued observation even when $|m|$ is already
large.  With $\rho>0$, a sufficiently strong signal is activated before its deployable
value evaporates; early activation is the correct response to decay.

Writing the obstacle as $g(t,m)=e^{-\rho t}\Phi(m,q(t))$ and the generator of the
posterior mean as $\mathcal L_tV=\tfrac12q(t)^2\partial_{mm}V$ (there is no first-order
term because $m_t$ is a martingale), the value solves the variational inequality
\begin{equation}
    \max\Bigl\{
        \partial_tV+\mathcal L_tV-c,\;
        g-V
    \Bigr\}=0,
    \qquad
    V(T,m)=g(T,m),
    \label{eq:tradeability_vi}
\end{equation}
with continuation region $\mathcal C=\{V>g\}$ and stopping region $\mathcal S=\{V=g\}$,
separated by a free boundary.  Because the obstacle is time dependent and the value can
kink at the boundary, we treat the variational inequality itself as the primary
object (the viscosity-solution view) rather than imposing smooth pasting by hand.

We solve \eqref{eq:tradeability_vi} by backward implicit Euler on a uniform $(t,m)$
grid, taking the pointwise maximum with the obstacle after each implicit step.  We pin
the spatial boundaries to the immediate stopping payoff, on a domain wide
enough that the computed free boundaries sit well inside it.  The discretization is monotone, stable,
and consistent, the scheme-side conditions of the convergence framework of
\citet{barlesSouganidis1991} for viscosity solutions.  We validate the solver against
the exact terminal threshold \eqref{eq:activation_threshold}, symmetry in $m$, the
zero-payoff limit, monotonicity in the observation cost, and early exercise when
$\rho>0$.
Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian_tradeability_boundary} shows the resulting policy: a
continuation band around $m=0$, widest early, flanked by activation at large $|m|$ and
rejection where neither trading nor further observation pays.

\section{Simulation Evidence and the Null-Signal Problem}
\label{sec:simulation}

Before turning to real data, we validate the stopping rule inside the model it is
built to solve.  We simulate 10{,}000 evidence paths $dY_t=\theta\,dt+dW_t$ at each of
seven true strengths $\theta$ equally spaced between $-0.3$ and $0.3$, each playing
the role of a realized $X$, and we update the posterior exactly with the Gaussian
formulas \eqref{eq:gaussian_benchmark}.
The experiment uses prior $X\sim N(0,0.20)$, risk aversion $\lambda=1$, residual
variance $\sigma_r^2=0.05$, hurdle $\kappa=0.005$, observation cost $c=0.005$, decay
$\rho=1$, and horizon $T=2$ years.  A policy that activates at
$\tau$ takes the posterior-optimal exposure
$w_\tau^\star=m_\tau/\bigl(\lambda(\sigma_r^2+q_\tau)\bigr)$ and is scored by its
realized economic value under the true strength,
\begin{equation}
    e^{-\rho\tau}
    \left(
        \theta w_\tau^\star-\frac{\lambda}{2}\sigma_r^2(w_\tau^\star)^2-\kappa
    \right)
    -c\tau,
    \label{eq:realized_value}
\end{equation}
and by $-c\tau$ if it rejects.  Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_policy_comparison} compares
the dynamic boundary with two natural baselines: immediate activation once the static
threshold \eqref{eq:activation_threshold} is crossed, and a fixed posterior
$z$-statistic rule.

The comparison delivers one positive and one negative result.  For strong signals
($|\theta|=0.3$) the dynamic boundary earns a mean realized value of about $0.019$,
far above both baselines: it defers activation until the posterior is precise enough
to size the position well.  But at $\theta=0$ the Gaussian rule activates 87.0\% of
paths, with mean value $-0.0156$.  This failure is built into the prior.  A Gaussian
prior assigns zero probability to the event $X=0$, so the posterior probability of a
dead signal is identically zero no matter how flat the evidence.  Over the validation
horizon the posterior mean wanders enough that most null paths cross the activation
boundary.  The remedy we develop next is a prior with an explicit dead state, backed
by a false-discovery penalty.

    \caption{Pathwise validation under the Gaussian evidence model.  The dynamic
    boundary improves realized value for large $|\theta|$ but over-activates null
    signals at $\theta=0$, because the Gaussian prior cannot represent a dead alpha;

\section{A Dead/Alive Prior with a False-Discovery Penalty}
\label{sec:deadalive}

\subsection{Three-state posterior}

We replace the Gaussian prior on the strength $X$ with the finite prior
\begin{equation}
    X\in\{-a,0,+a\},
    \qquad
    \mathbb P(X=-a)=p_-,\quad
    \mathbb P(X=0)=p_0,\quad
    \mathbb P(X=+a)=p_+,
    \label{eq:three_state_prior}
\end{equation}
whose states $\theta_i\in\{-a,0,+a\}$ have direct economic meanings: live short, dead,
live long.  Given evidence $Y_t=y$, the posterior weights are
\begin{equation}
    \pi_i(t,y)
    =
    \frac{p_i\exp\{\theta_iy-\theta_i^2t/2\}}
    {\sum_jp_j\exp\{\theta_jy-\theta_j^2t/2\}},
    \label{eq:three_state_posterior}
\end{equation}
with posterior mean $m(t,y)=\sum_i\pi_i\theta_i$ and variance
$q(t,y)=\sum_i\pi_i\theta_i^2-m^2$.  As in the EKV setup, the posterior mean is the
innovation drift,
\begin{equation}
    dY_t=m(t,Y_t)\,dt+d\widetilde W_t,
    \label{eq:three_state_innovation}
\end{equation}
which is the observation-filtration representation of the classical multi-hypothesis
sequential test.  The state now carries one extra economically meaningful coordinate,
the posterior dead probability $\pi_0(t,y)$.  If evidence hovers near zero while time
passes, the dead probability rises: a live alpha would probably have produced
directional evidence by then.  We check the implementation against five exact
identities of the exponential family: the weights sum to one; under a symmetric prior,
$\pi_+(t,y)=\pi_-(t,-y)$ and $m(t,y)=-m(t,-y)$; the dead probability $\pi_0(t,0)$
increases in $t$; the EKV identity $\partial_ym(t,y)=q(t,y)$ holds; and conditional
innovation increments are centered.

\subsection{Penalizing dead activations}

A dead state in the prior is necessary but not sufficient.  If the terminal reward
remains the pure trading payoff \eqref{eq:tradeability_payoff}, the model still admits
too many noisy null paths: conditional on a lucky posterior mean, trading looks
profitable even when the dead state is quite likely.  This behavior follows from the
objective rather than from the numerics: the payoff \eqref{eq:tradeability_payoff}
depends on the belief only through the posterior mean and variance, so it charges nothing
for the probability that an activated signal is dead.  A research process does bear
such costs, through capacity, monitoring, and operational risk.  We model them with a
false-discovery penalty $\eta\ge0$, paid when the signal is activated and $X=0$; its
posterior expectation at activation is $\eta\,\pi_0(t,y)$, so the obstacle becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_\eta(t,y)
    =
    e^{-\rho t}
    \left(
        \frac{m(t,y)^2}{2\lambda(\sigma_r^2+q(t,y))}-\kappa-\eta\,\pi_0(t,y)
    \right)^{\!+}.
    \label{eq:dead_alive_payoff}
\end{equation}
The function $\Phi_\eta$ plays the role of the discounted obstacle $g$ of
Section~\ref{sec:vi}, now with the penalty inside.  The value function in $(t,y)$
coordinates solves
\begin{equation}
    \max\Bigl\{
        \Phi_\eta(t,y)-V,\;
        \partial_tV+m(t,y)\,\partial_yV+\tfrac12\partial_{yy}V-c
    \Bigr\}=0,
    \qquad
    V(T,y)=\Phi_\eta(T,y).
    \label{eq:three_state_vi}
\end{equation}
The continuation operator is exactly the generator of
\eqref{eq:three_state_innovation}.  The solver discretizes it with monotone upwind
weights before applying the obstacle, a standard safeguard for viscosity solutions
of free-boundary problems.  The decision here is one step upstream of portfolio
choice: a portfolio model takes the signal as given and sizes positions, whereas this
model decides whether the signal enters the trading system at all.

\subsection{Controlled stress test}
\label{sec:deadalive_test}

We rerun the simulation experiment with a matched pair of rules solved at a common
prior scale: a three-state prior with dead probability $p_0=0.70$, symmetric live
states, and live magnitude $a=\sqrt{0.045/(1-p_0)}\approx0.387$, and a Gaussian
benchmark whose prior variance $0.045$ equals the three-state prior's second moment.
The penalty is $\eta=0.002$.  The trading payoff, decay, and horizon match
Section~\ref{sec:simulation}; the observation cost is $c=0.002$, and each strength
uses 20{,}000 paths.  The Gaussian null activation rate here (47.1\% below) differs
from Section~\ref{sec:simulation}'s 87.0\% because the prior variance and the
observation cost both differ; within this experiment the two rules share every
parameter, so the only difference between them is the explicit dead state and its
penalty.  The finite-difference diagnostics hold at machine precision: zero obstacle
violation, zero terminal-value error, and symmetry error $4.2\times10^{-16}$.  The
posterior dead probability at $y=0$ rises from $0.70$ to $0.731$ over the horizon, as
it should when no directional evidence arrives.

Figure~\ref{fig:dead_alive_policy_comparison} reports the policy comparison.  At
$\theta=0$ the dead/alive boundary lowers false activation from 47.1\% (matched
Gaussian boundary) to 39.8\% and improves null realized value from $-0.00560$ to
$-0.00513$.  This protection costs something: realized value on live alphas falls
slightly below the Gaussian benchmark.  What emerges is a disciplined tradeoff between
false discovery and power rather than a dominance claim, with the penalty $\eta$ as
the dial.

\section{Real-Data Evidence}
\label{sec:empirics}

\subsection{Anomaly portfolio data and protocol}
\label{sec:osap_protocol}

Our main experiment uses the Chen--Zimmermann Open Source Asset Pricing dataset,
specifically the Federal Reserve release of monthly long-short returns for 205
published predictors \citep{chenZimmermann2022}.\footnote{Data page:
\url{https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/open-source-cross-sectional-asset-pricing.htm}.}

We convert monthly returns into Brownian evidence increments.  Let
$\widehat\sigma_m$ be the monthly volatility estimated on a 120-month calibration
window.  With $dt=1/12$,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta Y_t=\frac{r_t}{\widehat\sigma_m}\sqrt{dt},
    \label{eq:evidence_increment}
\end{equation}
so that under a null signal $\operatorname{Var}(\Delta Y_t)\approx dt$, matching the
Brownian increment scale.  The implied drift is then an annualized Sharpe-like
evidence strength.  The protocol uses a common start date of January 1970: 120 months
of calibration, up to 240 months of sequential validation, and a 120-month
post-decision holdout window.  Of the 205 predictors, 192 have enough post-1970
history to qualify.  All OSAP experiments use risk aversion $\lambda=1$, residual
variance $\sigma_r^2=0.05$, hurdle $\kappa=0.005$, observation cost $c=0.001$, decay
$\rho=0.25$, and the three-state prior of Section~\ref{sec:deadalive_test}
($p_0=0.70$, $a\approx0.387$).  We solve the variational inequality on a
$241\times801$ grid covering the 20-year horizon and $|y|\le8$.

Our baseline placebo, the per-month sign flip, destroys drift while keeping each
signal's volatility profile: for every signal we independently flip the sign of each
monthly validation return with probability one half.  Per-month randomization matters
here.  A single global sign flip would leave absolute drift unchanged and a symmetric
rule unaffected, whereas independent flips zero the expected drift while preserving
the distribution of absolute returns.  The calibration below uses one fixed-seed draw per signal.  Across
200 independent redraws, the placebo activation rate averages 30.0\% with a standard
deviation of 3.0 points and a maximum of 37.0\%; the single-draw 27.1\% rate is
therefore typical, and the gap to the 72.9\% real rate is far outside placebo sampling
noise.

A stricter design, the block-flip placebo, draws one random sign per 12-month block,
which preserves within-year dependence of signed returns while still destroying
long-run drift.  This placebo activates 50.5\% of the time on average across 200
redraws and never exceeds 60.4\%, below the real rate in every draw but well above the
per-month placebo.  The comparison is informative about what the rule certifies.  At
the rule's typical four-year decision time, sign-randomized year-long drift segments
are locally hard to distinguish from genuine drift; the admission decision therefore
responds to directional persistence at horizons beyond a year, not only to full-sample
drift.

We calibrate the false-discovery penalty by sweeping $\eta$ over the real and
placebo panels (Figure~\ref{fig:osap_penalty_sweep}).  With $\eta=0$ the rule activates
91.1\% of real signals but also 60.4\% of placebos; raising $\eta$ lowers both rates,
the placebo rate faster.  We adopt $\eta=0.04$, a conservative choice in the middle of
the sweep: real activation stays high while placebo activation falls below one third.

Selecting $\eta$ out of sample gives the same answer.  In 25 replications that
calibrate $\eta$ on one independent placebo draw (the smallest grid value with placebo
activation below one third) and evaluate on a fresh draw, the selected penalty ranges
over $0.03$--$0.05$ with median $0.04$.  The out-of-sample placebo rate averages
31.0\% (95\% range 21.9\% to 39.0\%), and real activation stays between 69.3\% and
77.1\%.  The cross-method comparisons that follow hold $\eta=0.04$ fixed across all
rules.

\subsection{Sequential versus fixed-horizon admission}
\label{sec:osap_results}

At $\eta=0.04$ the sequential rule activates 140 of 192 real predictors (72.9\%) and
52 of 192 placebos (27.1\%).  Among eight canonical signals (short-term reversal,
momentum, book-to-market, operating profitability, asset growth, gross profitability,
accruals, and bid-ask spread), it activates six and rejects two; the activated group
earns an average holdout return of 1.30\% per month over the next 120 months.  Because
the placebo preserves each signal's volatility profile, the 45.8-point real--placebo
gap shows that persistent direction, not volatility alone, drives most activations.

The sharper question is whether the result is genuinely sequential or a fixed-horizon
posterior test in disguise.  We therefore compare against fixed-horizon rules at 12,
24, 60, 120, and 240 validation months.  Each fixed rule uses the same three-state
posterior and is calibrated on the same placebo panel to activate exactly 52 placebos,
matching the sequential rule's placebo rate.  In this symmetric model the three
candidate scores (posterior net payoff, posterior $z$-statistic, and posterior alive
probability) rank signals identically at a fixed horizon, since each is monotone in
absolute evidence strength; we verify the agreement numerically.

Figure~\ref{fig:osap_static_benchmark} reports the comparison.  The sequential rule
activates 72.9\% of real signals with a mean decision time of 47.9 months and a mean
holdout return of 0.747\% per month among activated signals.  The 12- and 24-month
tests activate only 41.7\% and 40.6\% of real signals and are not competitive.
Closest in timing is the fixed 60-month test, which is weaker on both margins (70.8\%,
0.705\%).  Longer fixed tests buy more activation (77.1\% at 120 months, 82.8\% at 240
months) but require waiting 10 or 20 years, and their activated holdout returns fall
to 0.689\% and 0.537\%.  On the summary metric of activation-weighted holdout return
(activation rate times mean activated holdout return), the sequential rule attains
0.545 against 0.500, 0.531, and 0.444 for the 60-, 120-, and 240-month tests.  The
sequential rule does not dominate on raw activation count; its advantage is timing,
coming within ten points of the 240-month test's power while deciding in a fifth of
the time.

Two caveats qualify this comparison.  First, decision times differ across rules, so
holdout windows sample different calendar periods, and because anomaly returns decay
after publication \citep{mcleanPontiff2016}, part of the 240-month test's weaker
holdout performance may reflect calendar drift rather than the horizon itself.

Second, the holdout differences carry wide confidence bands.  We bootstrap them in
two ways with 2{,}000 replications each, holding the placebo calibration fixed.  The
first design resamples circular 12-month calendar blocks jointly across signals,
holding decisions fixed and preserving cross-signal correlation; the second resamples
the 192 signals with replacement, decisions included.  The sequential gain in
activation-weighted holdout return is $+0.045$ over the 60-month test (time-block
95\% interval $-0.002$ to $0.099$; cluster $0.011$ to $0.081$), $+0.013$ over the
120-month test (insignificant in both designs), and $+0.100$ over the 240-month test
(time-block $-0.126$ to $0.313$; cluster $0.015$ to $0.188$).  The point estimates
are positive throughout but not distinguishable from zero under the conservative
calendar-time design.  We therefore read the post-decision comparison as suggestive;
the unambiguous results are the real-versus-placebo separation and the earlier
decision time.

\subsection{Numerical and specification robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:grid_convergence} checks the stability of the variational-inequality
solver for the three-state OSAP configuration against a fine
$481\times1001$ reference grid.  The paper grid has an initial-value error below
$9\times10^{-5}$ and under 1\% region disagreement on a common time--evidence
evaluation grid; its activation boundaries sit within a few hundredths of a Brownian
evidence unit of the fine-grid boundaries.  The results are economically stable rather than exact to the last
grid point.

The admission pattern is not specific to one parameter point.  Halving or doubling
$\kappa$, $c$, and $\rho$ one at a time moves real activation between 47\% and 81\%
and the mean decision time between 25 and 71 months, with the expected comparative
statics: costlier observation and faster decay shorten validation and admit fewer
signals.  The real--placebo activation gap stays between 25 and 53 points across all
seven configurations.

The Brownian evidence approximation is imperfect: across eligible signals, the mean
validation increment variance divided by $dt$ is 1.24, and the median absolute lag-one
autocorrelation is 0.10.  These deviations are moderate but not negligible;
signal-specific volatility updating is a natural refinement.

\subsection{A stock-level feasibility check}
\label{sec:wrds}

The OSAP experiments use pre-packaged portfolio returns.  As a feasibility check on
the full pipeline, we rebuild momentum, book-to-market, and operating profitability
from raw CRSP and Compustat stock-level data on WRDS \citep{wrdsPlatform}: 3.30
million CRSP stock-months, 8.55 million merged stock-signal-months, and 2{,}040
monthly long-short portfolio returns.  Each month we form value-weighted
top-minus-bottom decile portfolios from common stocks, use next-month returns
including delisting returns, and lag accounting data by six months.  We run the same
admission protocol on gross returns and on a punitive net-return stress test that
subtracts turnover times a monthly high-low range cost proxy
(Table~\ref{tab:wrds_stock_level}).  This experiment is a demonstration, not a
measurement: three known-positive signals cannot discriminate among rules, and the
range proxy removes 13.20\%, 5.15\%, and 3.08\% per month from momentum,
book-to-market, and operating profitability, deliberately harsher than the
effective-spread approach used in anomaly net-return studies such as
\citet{chenVelikov2023}.

Both arms behave as expected.  On gross returns, the rule admits all three signals
in the standard direction, with a mean standard-direction holdout return of 1.227\%
per month, the expected outcome for three of the best-documented anomalies.  Under the
punitive cost model, the posterior concentrates on strongly negative net drift, and
the symmetric rule ``activates'' by shorting the net return stream.  Reversing a
costly portfolio is not the same as receiving the negative of its net return, so the
economically meaningful count is standard-direction admissions, and it is zero.  The
takeaway is methodological: the pipeline runs end to end on raw data, and the
admission decision moves from full acceptance to full rejection as the cost model
varies.  A credible stock-level deployment therefore needs measured effective spreads
rather than a proxy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The standard response to the anomaly zoo is to keep the fixed-length test and raise
its threshold.  This paper instead makes the test length itself the object of choice.
We model the accumulating evidence about a candidate signal with the EKV filtering
framework, attach an economic terminal payoff that nets risk-adjusted profit against
an implementation hurdle and a false-discovery penalty, and compute an optimal
continue/activate/reject policy by solving a variational inequality.

The evidence supports this formulation at three levels.  In simulation, the sequential
boundary outperforms static thresholds on strong signals, and the dead/alive prior cuts
false activation on dead ones.  On 192 anomaly portfolios, the rule activates 72.9\% of
real signals against 27.1\% of sign-flip placebos, and it reaches within ten points of
a 240-month test's activation power while deciding in 48 months on average.  At the
stock level, the pipeline runs end to end on raw CRSP/Compustat data, and the
admission decision swings from full acceptance to full rejection with the assumed
trading costs.

Three limitations remain, and each points to a concrete extension.  First, the
block-flip placebo shows that the rule certifies persistent direction over multi-year
stretches, not long-run drift alone, and the evidence model cannot tell whether that
persistence is alpha or slow-moving style exposure.  Second, the Brownian evidence
model is an approximation; volatility updating, heavier-tailed evidence, and an
exposure-dependent cost $\kappa_0+\kappa_1|w|+\kappa_2w^2$ are the natural
refinements.  Third, the stock-level experiment covers three signals under a punitive
cost proxy; repeating it across the anomaly zoo with measured effective spreads would
turn the stress test into a measurement.  The longer-term goal is a research process
that validates many candidate signals at once, under a shared false-discovery budget,
and activates each one when its accumulated evidence warrants it rather than when a
fixed calendar ends.
